KEPT
Keep your commitments. Grow your savings.

1. PRODUCT THESIS

Kept is a consumer behavioural-savings product built with blockchain infrastructure that is intentionally kept out of the user's way.

The user experience is simple:

Create a savings goal
→ Add money
→ Choose predefined commitments
→ Complete those commitments in real life
→ Verify completion
→ Earn additional savings rewards
→ Build consistency and save more

The product is not presented as a DeFi application. Users should not need to understand wallets, bridges, gas, chain IDs, smart contracts, solver networks, attestations, or even Monad in order to use it.

The core idea is:

Kept rewards people not only for saving money, but for following through on useful real-world commitments that support the life they are trying to build.

Blockchain is infrastructure, not the product.

2. POSITIONING

Consumer pitch:

Kept helps you save toward something that matters and rewards you for keeping the commitments that help you get there.

Technical pitch:

Kept converts privacy-preserving proofs of real-world behaviour into savings incentives on Monad, using Privy for programmable invisible accounts and Aurora Intents / NEAR Intents to bring liquidity from supported chains into the product.

Tagline:

Keep your commitments. Grow your savings.

A useful internal product statement:

Kept does not onboard people to Monad. It onboards people to saving.

3. WHY THE PRODUCT EXISTS

Traditional savings products mainly reward capital already deposited.

Kept adds a behavioural incentive layer.

A user might create:

Savings goal:
Save £1,000 for a laptop.

Selected commitments:
- Save £25 each week.
- Complete three study sessions each week.
- Make no withdrawals for 30 days.

The user's underlying savings yield is not taken away when they miss a commitment.

Instead:

Base yield = the return generated by the savings product.
Behavioural reward = an additional, bounded incentive for completing verified commitments.

The system is designed to encourage behaviour rather than punish failure.

The underlying technologies should become visible only where transparency, security, or advanced settings make that useful.

5. USER ACCOUNTS — PRIVY

Privy is a core infrastructure dependency, not merely an authentication provider.

Privy should provide:

- Familiar sign-in such as email, social login and passkeys.
- Automatic embedded EVM wallet provisioning.
- The user's underlying Monad account.
- Transaction signing.
- Monad gas sponsorship.
- MFA/passkey authorization for sensitive actions.
- Wallet policies.
- External wallet linking where useful.
- Recovery/security functionality.

Removing Privy should materially break major product functionality. It is the account, authorization, security and transaction layer.

MVP savings deposits require an explicit user-authorized transaction. Privy may sponsor that Monad transaction, but Kept does not retain delegated saving authority or schedule account actions.

The application must never receive unrestricted authority over the user's wallet. Deposit authorization must bind the asset, amount, destination vault and receiver.

6. FUNDING KEPT

Kept should eventually support three broad funding paths through a single "Add money" experience:

A. Fiat
- Bank transfer
- Card or supported regulated on-ramp rails
- Fiat is converted into the asset Kept requires underneath the interface
- KYC/compliance remains with an appropriate regulated provider rather than being built by Kept

B. Crypto on another chain
- USDC on Base
- ETH on Ethereum
- SOL on Solana
- Other assets and networks supported by Aurora / NEAR Intents
- Aurora Intents / NEAR Intents abstracts the cross-chain route into Monad

C. Assets already on Monad
- Directly usable without cross-chain routing

The user-facing action remains:

Add money

not:

Bridge to Monad.

7. AURORA INTENTS / NEAR INTENTS

Aurora Intents, powered by NEAR Intents, provides the cross-chain liquidity and solver-based execution layer.

Its role is to make the source of the user's crypto capital largely irrelevant.

Example:

User has:
300 USDC on Base

User wants:
300 USDC worth of savings on Kept

Kept presents:
Add 300 USDC

Underneath:
Base USDC
→ Aurora / NEAR Intents
→ Monad-compatible savings asset
→ Kept

The user should not be sent to a separate traditional bridge interface.

Longer term, the reverse should also work:

Withdraw
→ Bank account, or
→ Preferred supported crypto asset/network

8. MONAD'S ROLE

Monad is the financial execution and settlement layer.

Monad should hold or verify financially relevant state such as:

- Savings positions.
- Deposits.
- Withdrawals.
- Commitment identifiers.
- Qualification results.
- Reward weights.
- Reward claims.
- Reward pools.
- Protocol accounting.

Monad should not hold raw behavioural or personal information simply because it can.

9. SAVINGS GOALS

Users create personal savings pots.

Examples:
- Emergency fund
- Holiday
- Laptop
- Education
- House deposit
- Personal project
- General savings

A savings goal can contain:

- User-defined name.
- Target amount.
- Target date.
- Current balance.
- Progress.
- Contribution history.
- Base yield.
- Current behavioural reward.
- Active commitments.
- Current streak.
- Reward history.

The savings goal can be freely named.

The reward-bearing behavioural commitments cannot be freely written.

10. CURATED COMMITMENT CATALOGUE

Users must NOT define arbitrary reward-bearing goals through free text.

Instead, they select from a curated catalogue of predefined commitment types.

This is a deliberate safety, privacy, anti-abuse and product-design choice.

Kept defines:

- What the commitment means.
- Which parameters users may configure.
- What counts as success.
- What proof methods are acceptable.
- How much reward weight it can carry.
- What privacy rules apply.
- What abuse risks exist.
- Whether it is eligible for financial rewards.

Core principle:

Users choose their commitments. Kept defines what constitutes proof.

11. EXAMPLE COMMITMENT CATEGORIES

Financial commitments:
- Save a defined amount weekly.
- Save a defined amount monthly.
- Make no withdrawals for 7/14/30 days.
- Maintain a minimum balance.
- Complete a recurring contribution schedule.

Fitness commitments:
- Complete a defined number of qualifying workouts.
- Reach a predefined activity target.
- Maintain an activity streak.

Learning commitments:
- Complete a defined number of study sessions.
- Complete supported course milestones.
- Complete defined learning activity.

Career commitments:
- Complete predefined career-development sessions.
- Complete approved job-search activity.
- Complete professional-learning milestones.

Development/creative commitments:
- Complete predefined contribution or project milestones where suitable proof adapters exist.

Not every desirable life goal needs to carry a financial reward.

Unverifiable or highly subjective activities can exist as personal tracking commitments with little or no reward eligibility.

12. COMMITMENT DEFINITIONS

Each commitment type should have a machine-readable internal definition.

Example:

Commitment type:
WEEKLY_SAVINGS

Parameters:
amount = £25
period = 7 days

Verification:
onchain

Maximum reward weight:
1.0

Another:

Commitment type:
FITNESS_WORKOUT_COUNT

Parameters:
target = 3
period = 7 days

Verification:
external proof and/or social verification

Maximum reward weight:
0.7

Users may configure allowed parameters, but cannot redefine the semantics of the commitment.

13. VERIFICATION CLASSES

Kept should recognize that different behaviours have different levels of provability.

Class A — Objective / onchain proof

Examples:
- Make a deposit.
- Maintain balance.
- Make no withdrawal.
- Complete a recurring savings contribution.

Highest confidence.

Class B — External proof

Examples:
- Fitness activity from a supported provider.
- GitHub activity.
- Supported educational activity.
- Other trusted integrations.

High confidence where the source is reliable.

Class C — Social verification

Trusted people confirm completion when technical proof is impractical.

This carries lower confidence than strong objective evidence.

Class D — Self-report

Useful for tracking but carries little or no protocol-funded financial reward.

14. PRIVACY PRINCIPLE

Kept should follow:

Prove enough to qualify. Reveal as little as possible.

The blockchain should usually know only that a qualifying commitment was completed to a given confidence threshold.

It does not need to know what sensitive real-world evidence produced that result.

15. MINIMUM-DISCLOSURE PROOF

Raw evidence should not be shown to verifiers by default.

For example, a fitness commitment must not unnecessarily disclose:

- Exact gym.
- GPS coordinates.
- Home/work travel patterns.
- Workout timestamps.
- Personal health data.

Instead, the system should attempt to produce:

"Weekly fitness target satisfied."

The verifier should learn only what is needed to answer the verification question.

16. PROOF ADAPTERS

Proof adapters transform raw evidence into standardized minimal claims.

Examples:

Fitness data
→ Fitness proof adapter
→ "Weekly fitness target satisfied"

GitHub data
→ Development proof adapter
→ "Contribution threshold satisfied"

Monad savings data
→ Savings proof adapter
→ "Weekly deposit completed"

The behavioural reward engine consumes the normalized result, not the raw evidence.

Where possible, raw information should remain on-device or with the originating trusted service rather than being copied into Kept.

17. EVIDENCE AND LOCATION PRIVACY

Kept must not solve behavioural verification by casually handing raw evidence to friends.

A user's friend should not need to learn where their gym is, where they study, where they work, or where they attended a sensitive appointment.

For location-based evidence, the desired future model is a predicate:

"User visited the precommitted qualifying location three times."

not:

"User visited [specific location] at [specific time]."

Future privacy technology could include:
- Commitments/hashes.
- Selective disclosure.
- Zero-knowledge proofs.
- Local proof generation.

The MVP should not force unnecessary zero-knowledge complexity, but its architecture should not prevent future migration toward stronger privacy.

18. SOCIAL VERIFICATION

Some predefined commitment types may use trusted human verification.

Example verifier prompt:

"Did Jordan complete the agreed weekly study commitment?"

Available response:
- Yes
- No

The verifier should not automatically see:
- Savings balance.
- Target savings amount.
- Yield.
- Wallet address.
- Other commitments.
- Other verifiers.
- Raw evidence.
- Sensitive location information.

19. PRIVATE VERIFICATION PROMPTS

The private context of a commitment and the prompt given to a verifier should be separated where possible.

The verifier only receives the minimum context required to provide a meaningful response.

Users should control what selected verifiers can see within the constraints Kept requires for a valid commitment.

20. PRIVATE SOCIAL GRAPH

Kept should not accidentally publish a social graph onchain.

If Alice publicly attests to Jordan's commitments every week, blockchain analysis could reveal their relationship.

Preferred model:

- Individual verifier attestations are signed offchain.
- The verification/trust service checks them.
- Monad receives the financially relevant qualification result.
- Where useful, an epoch commitment/Merkle root can provide later auditability without exposing all individual relationships.

Monad might receive:

Commitment ID: 82719
Qualified: true
Confidence: 0.82
Reward weight: 0.82

It should not need to receive:

Alice and Bob verified Jordan's private health goal.

21. ANTI-ABUSE THESIS

Kept will never be perfectly fraud-proof.

The goal is:

Make abuse uneconomic at scale while keeping legitimate use pleasant.

The system should combine:

- Capped rewards.
- Real savings capital.
- Time-weighted balances.
- Account history.
- Verifier reputation.
- Social graph independence.
- Collusion detection.
- Random challenges.
- Objective proof where available.
- Limited reward-bearing commitments.
- Confidence-weighted verification.

22. SOCIAL VERIFICATION IS WEIGHTED, NOT BINARY

Two friends clicking "Yes" should not automatically equal unquestionable truth.

Kept should calculate confidence.

Conceptually:

Goal evidence
+ verifier reputation
+ social graph independence
+ account history
- abuse signals
= verification confidence

The financial system consumes the confidence/qualification result.

23. COLLUSION AND SYBIL SIGNALS

Potential warning signals include:

- High reciprocal verification.
- Small closed groups that only verify one another.
- Very high approval rates.
- Repeated identical verifier sets.
- Newly created accounts clustered together.
- Low relationship diversity.
- Circular verification patterns.
- Unusually rapid verification behaviour.
- Repeated failed audits or disputes.

Suspicious users do not necessarily need to be immediately banned.

A better response may be:

"Additional verification required."

Their verification weight can be reduced until stronger evidence exists.

24. VERIFIER REPUTATION

Verifier credibility can develop over time.

Potential inputs:
- Account age.
- Number of unique users verified.
- Network diversity.
- Approval/rejection behaviour.
- Reciprocity.
- Disputes.
- Successful challenges/audits.
- History across commitment categories.

A verifier who approves everything becomes less credible.

Kept should not pay users directly per verification, because that would create verification farms.

25. RANDOM CHALLENGES

A small percentage of socially verified commitments may require additional confirmation.

Possible challenge methods:
- Another independent verifier.
- A supported external proof source.
- A privacy-reduced proof.
- A proof-adapter result.

The purpose is deterrence, not constant surveillance.

Raw evidence should remain a last resort and should be minimized, redacted and ephemeral if it is ever required.

26. REWARD ECONOMICS

Recommended structure:

Base savings yield
+
Capped behavioural reward

Illustrative example:

Underlying savings yield: 4.5%
Behavioural reward: +0% to +2.0%
Maximum effective rate: 6.5%

The behavioural reward pool should be independently budgeted and ideally pre-funded for each reward period.

Potential future funding sources:
- Protocol revenue.
- Sponsor incentives.
- Partner campaigns.
- Employer programmes.
- University/education programmes.
- Wellness programmes.
- Fintech partners.
- Public-interest programmes.

27. REWARD EPOCHS

A practical model:

Weekly commitments.
Monthly behavioural reward settlement.

This reduces settlement complexity and allows the protocol to know its maximum reward liability in advance.

28. TIME-WEIGHTED ELIGIBLE BALANCE

Behavioural rewards should be calculated against savings genuinely committed over time.

Do not simply use the balance at the end of the reward period.

Example:

Week 1: £500
Week 2: £750
Week 3: £750
Week 4: £1,000

Reward basis:
approximately the average eligible balance, not simply £1,000.

29. ELIGIBLE BALANCE CAPS

Behavioural rewards should apply only to a capped portion of a user's savings.

Example:

Total savings: £50,000
Behaviour-reward eligible balance: £2,000

The full balance may earn base yield, while only the capped amount earns protocol-subsidized behavioural rewards.

This dramatically reduces the value of manipulating the behavioural system.

30. REWARD FORMULA

A simplified conceptual model:

Behavioural Reward =
Average Eligible Balance
× Maximum Behavioural Rate
× Completion Score
× Verification Confidence
× Epoch Duration

The actual production model can later include caps and consistency/streak modifiers.

31. LIMITED ACTIVE REWARD COMMITMENTS

Users should be limited to a small number of reward-bearing commitments per period.

Reasons:
- Prevent goal farming.
- Keep the experience focused.
- Reduce fraud surface.
- Make behavioural incentives meaningful.

Users can still track additional personal commitments without financial rewards.

32. STREAKS

Consistency can modestly increase rewards.

Illustrative model:

1 week: 1.00x
4 weeks: 1.05x
8 weeks: 1.10x
12+ weeks: 1.15x maximum

Streaks should not produce extreme multipliers.

Missing one period should not erase months of progress. The product should encourage recovery rather than abandonment.

33. WITHDRAWAL DESIGN

Funds must remain under the user's control.

Kept can introduce behavioural friction:

"Withdrawing now will reduce your current reward rate and affect your savings streak."

But it should not trap a user's money.

Privy passkey/MFA can add protection for large or unusual withdrawals.

34. ACCOUNTABILITY CIRCLES

Longer-term social functionality can include private accountability circles.

Example:

Emergency Fund Circle

Jordan: 45%
Alice: 72%
Bob: 63%

Users control what progress information is shared.

Circle members may:
- Encourage one another.
- Participate in shared challenges.
- Verify eligible commitments.
- Share limited progress.

Members never have custody over another member's funds.

35. PRIVACY DEFAULTS

Financial and behavioural information is private by default.

Possible visibility levels:
- Private.
- Trusted verifiers only.
- Selected circle.
- Explicitly public.

Full balances should never be public by default.

36. FIAT ON-RAMP AND OFF-RAMP VISION

Kept should eventually support normal-money entry and exit.

Add money:
- Bank
- Card
- Crypto

Withdraw:
- Bank
- Crypto

Regulated payment/on-ramp providers should own the relevant KYC/compliance process.

Kept should orchestrate those rails rather than becoming its own banking or on-ramp provider.

This allows a completely non-crypto user to experience:

Google/passkey sign-in
→ Add £100
→ Save toward emergency fund
→ Complete commitments
→ Earn rewards
→ Withdraw pounds

without consciously interacting with blockchain technology.

37. BROADER PLATFORM OPPORTUNITY

Kept can grow beyond a direct-to-consumer savings app.

Potential sponsored programmes:

Employers:
"Build a £500 emergency fund and we will match £50."

Universities:
"Complete approved learning milestones and unlock a savings contribution."

Training providers:
"Complete the programme and receive a matched savings reward."

Wellness partners:
"Complete an approved activity programme and earn a savings contribution."

These models can fund more meaningful rewards than a permanent protocol-subsidized APY boost.

This turns Kept into infrastructure for financially incentivized behavioural programmes.

41. HACKATHON MVP

The MVP should prove one polished end-to-end savings loop rather than attempting every future feature.

It must also implement exactly three initial reward-bearing commitment types so the MVP demonstrates Kept's three core verification paths rather than presenting social verification as the whole product:

A. Objective / onchain:
- Weekly savings commitment.
- Example: "Save £25 this week."
- Verified from Kept's onchain savings activity/accounting.

B. External proof adapter:
- Verified activity-count commitment.
- Example: "Complete 3 qualifying activity sessions this week."
- Verified through a privacy-minimized external proof adapter.

C. Social verification:
- Study-session commitment.
- Example: "Complete 3 study sessions this week."
- Verified through private offchain attestations and simplified trust/confidence scoring.

The catalogue, backend, UX and test suite must support all three commitment types in the MVP.

Target saver flow:

1. User signs in through Privy.
2. Privy provisions the embedded Monad account.
3. User creates a savings goal.
4. User funds the goal, including one cross-chain path through Aurora / NEAR Intents.
5. User selects one of the three predefined MVP commitment types.
6. User completes the commitment.
7. Kept routes verification through the appropriate path:
   - objective/onchain evaluation;
   - external proof adapter; or
   - private social verification.
8. For social verification, trusted verifiers receive privacy-minimized requests and sign offchain attestations.
9. For external verification, the proof adapter reduces provider data to the minimum qualification result required by Kept.
10. A simplified trust/verification layer produces the financially relevant qualification/confidence result.
11. Monad records the minimal qualification result required for reward settlement.
12. Behavioural reward is unlocked from the separate pre-funded reward treasury.
13. Privy sponsors the relevant Monad transaction.
14. User sees updated savings and reward progress.

KEPT-PL-023 removes delegated and scheduled automatic saving from the MVP.

MVP savings deposits require an explicit user-authorized transaction. Privy may provide the embedded account and sponsor the Monad transaction, but Kept does not retain a delegated savings permission, run an automatic-savings scheduler or expose an automation-specific vault function.

42. HACKATHON ANTI-ABUSE DEMO

A useful demo should also show that social verification is not blindly trusted.

Two new, highly reciprocal accounts repeatedly verify one another.

The verification confidence remains below the required threshold.

This proves the trust layer is part of the product rather than an afterthought.

45. CORE PRODUCT PRINCIPLES

1. Blockchain should be invisible.
The user is using a savings product, not operating blockchain infrastructure.

2. Users choose commitments; Kept defines proof.
No ambiguous free-text reward goals.

3. Privacy by default.
Reveal only what is necessary.

4. Incentives encourage rather than punish.
Users retain base yield and access to their funds.

5. Abuse should be uneconomic.
Use caps, real savings, time weighting, confidence, reputation and anti-collusion signals.

6. Social relationships provide accountability, never custody.
A verifier cannot access another user's funds.

7. Sensitive evidence should not be casually exposed.
Proof should be reduced to minimal predicates wherever possible.

8. Build the product, not the hackathon checklist.
The hackathon is a launch opportunity for a product intended to have life beyond the competition.

46. FINAL PRODUCT STATEMENT

Kept is a privacy-first behavioural savings product where users save toward goals and select predefined real-world commitments that can increase their rewards when credibly completed.

Privy makes the account and Monad interactions feel like normal consumer fintech.

Aurora / NEAR Intents makes the chain holding the user's existing crypto largely irrelevant.

Monad provides transparent financial settlement.

A privacy-preserving proof and trust layer connects real-life action to financial incentives without unnecessarily exposing the user's personal behaviour or social graph.

The result should feel simple:

Save money.
Keep your commitments.
Earn more for following through.
